The Mount Everest

Geographical & Physical Profile

• Official Height: 8,848.86 meters (29,031.7 feet). This definitive elevation was jointly surveyed, verified, and officially declared by the sovereign governments of Nepal and China.

• Location & Sovereignty: Situated in the Mahalangur Himal sub-range of the Himalayas. The exact international border line between Nepal (Koshi Province) and China (Tibet Autonomous Region) runs directly through its summit point.

• Tectonic Dynamics: The mountain continues to grow at an approximate rate of 4 millimeters per year due to the ongoing northward collision of the Indian tectonic plate into the Eurasian plate.

• Nomenclature:
• Nepal: Sagarmāthā (meaning "Peak of Heaven" or "Sky Head").
• Tibet: Chomolungma / Qomolangma (meaning "Goddess Mother of the World").
• Western History: Designated as "Peak XV" by the Great Trigonometric Survey in 1856 before being officially renamed in 1865 in honor of Sir George Everest, the British Surveyor General of India.

Strategic Climbing Routes & Logistics

There are two primary commercial mountaineering routes utilized to climb Mount Everest, detailed and compared below:

Attribute Southeast Ridge (Nepal Side) Northeast Ridge (Tibet/China Side)
Popularity Most popular route, utilized by approximately 70% of climbers. Less crowded but more heavily restricted and regulated by Chinese authorities.
Base Camp Elevation 5,364 meters (17,598 feet) 5,150 meters (16,900 feet)

Logistical Access Requires a multi-day trek in from Lukla Airport. Accessible directly by a paved road leading to the camp.

Historic Mountaineering Milestones

• 1922 (First 8,000m+ Threshold): Members of a British expedition became the first humans to climb above 8,000 meters, reaching 8,320 meters on the North Ridge while testing early supplemental oxygen systems.

• 1953 (First Confirmed Summit): Sir Edmund Hillary (New Zealand) and Tenzing Norgay (Sherpa, Nepal) achieved the first verified, successful ascent on May 29, 1953, climbing via the Southeast Ridge route.

• 1975 (First Female Ascent): Junko Tabei of Japan shattered gender barriers by becoming the first woman to officially reach the summit.

• 1978 (First Ascent Without Supplemental Oxygen): Reinhold Messner (Italy) and Peter Habeler (Austria) completed the near-impossible feat of summiting without oxygen. Messner followed this up by completing the first solo ascent in 1980.

Environmental Parameters & The 'Death Zone'

• The Death Zone: The region extending above 8,000 meters (26,247 feet) is universally designated as the 'Death Zone'. At this altitude, atmospheric pressure drops drastically, leaving available oxygen at just one-third of sea-level capacity. The human body cannot adapt and slowly deteriorates over time.

• Extreme Temperatures: Summit temperatures are highly volatile, averaging around -19°C (-2°F) during the warmest climbing window (May) and plummeting well below -36°C (-33°F) during peak winter months.

• Atmospheric Wind Speeds: Hurricane-force winds regularly exceed 160 km/h (100 mph) when the high-altitude jet stream dips southward, impacting the upper slopes.

Modern Administrative Regulations

To counteract dangerous overcrowdings and mitigate hazards, the Government of Nepal enforces a strict minimum experience standard. All applicants seeking an Everest climbing permit must prove they have successfully summited an official peak of at least 7,000 meters within Nepal. This regulatory policy filters out under-prepared individuals, prioritizing safety across commercial routes.
